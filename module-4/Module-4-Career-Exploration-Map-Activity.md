# Career Exploration Map Activity

This activity asks you to research at least three careers in depth using professional tools. You will build a Career Exploration Map — a structured, comparative overview of the careers you find most compelling. This becomes a reference document you will use in Section 4.5 when building your Career Plan.

---

## Step 1 — Select Three Careers to Research

Based on your RIASEC profile, your self-assessment, and your Ideation Intentions, identify three careers you want to explore in depth.

💡 **Guidance on selection:** Choose careers that genuinely interest you — even if you are uncertain about them.
- One should be a career you have considered seriously.
- One can be a career that surprised you in your RIASEC results.
- One can be a career that feels like a stretch but has always intrigued you.

## Career 1: Data Scientist

**Source(s) used:**
- [x ] O*NET
- [ ] BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook
- [ x] LinkedIn
- [x ] Other

What people in this career actually do day-to-day:
Data scientists collect, clean, analyze, and interpret large amounts of data to find patterns and solve problems. They use statistics, machine learning, data modeling, and visualization tools to make predictions and provide recommendations. They may also identify problems that can be solved through data, build and test predictive models, present their findings to decision-makers, and communicate what the data means. O*NET specifically identifies tasks such as analyzing large datasets, creating visualizations, testing models, presenting results, and recommending data-driven solutions.
Work environment:
Data scientists usually work in professional office or technology environments and spend a large amount of time using computers and sitting. They can work individually and as part of teams and communicate with managers, other technical professionals, and stakeholders. O*NET reports that 91% of respondents work indoors in environmentally controlled settings every day and that 76% spend most or almost all of their time sitting.
Required education and training:
A bachelor's degree is a common entry point, although many employers prefer or require graduate education for some positions. O*NET reports that 48% of respondents indicated a bachelor's degree and 44% indicated a master's degree as the education required for new hires. Additional training in statistics, programming, machine learning, databases, and data visualization would be useful.
Key skills and abilities required:
Mathematics
Critical thinking
Reading comprehension
Active learning
Speaking
These are among the essential skills identified by O*NET for data scientists.
Salary range:
Entry-level: approximately $63,650
Median: $112,590
Experienced: approximately $194,410
The entry-level and experienced figures represent the 10th and 90th percentiles of May 2024 wages, while the median was $112,590.
Job outlook:
Faster than average
Projected growth rate: 34% from 2024–2034.
The BLS projects 34% employment growth for data scientists, with about 23,400 openings per year on average.
Industries and employers that hire for this role:
Technology and software companies

Healthcare and scientific research
Retail and food companies
Government and nonprofit organizations

## Career 2: Software Developer

**Source(s) used:**

What people in this career actually do day-to-day:
Software developers design, build, test, maintain, and improve computer applications and software systems. They analyze what users need, develop technical solutions, work with other developers and engineers, test systems, fix problems, and communicate about project requirements. O*NET also identifies activities such as analyzing user needs, designing software, testing systems, modifying existing software, and collaborating with other professionals.
Work environment:
Software developers generally work indoors in office or technology environments and spend most of their time sitting at a computer. They often work on teams with other developers, engineers, managers, designers, or business professionals. O*NET reports that 95% of respondents spend almost all or all of their time sitting and that working with a team is extremely important for 71% of respondents.
Required education and training:
A bachelor's degree in computer science, information technology, or a related field is typically the most common route. BLS states that software developers typically need a bachelor's degree in computer and information technology or a related field. Some employers prefer candidates with master's degrees.
Key skills and abilities required:
Critical thinking
Active learning
Reading comprehension
Active listening
Writing
O*NET also identifies programming, systems analysis, complex problem-solving, technology design, and systems evaluation as important transferable skills.

## Career 3: Operations Research Analyst

**Source(s) used:**
- [ ] O*NET
- [ ] BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook
- [ x] LinkedIn
- [ x] Other

What people in this career actually do day-to-day:
Operations research analysts use mathematics, data, models, and analytical methods to help organizations make better decisions. They identify problems, gather and validate information, build mathematical or simulation models, compare possible solutions, analyze logistics, and make recommendations to decision-makers. O*NET specifically includes logistics networks, decision-support systems, optimization, project planning, and resource allocation among the work of these professionals.
Work environment:
Operations research analysts generally work in offices and spend significant amounts of time working with computers and data. They often collaborate with managers and other professionals and may present their findings to decision-makers. O*NET reports that 86% work indoors in environmentally controlled settings every day and 71% spend most or almost all of their time sitting.
Required education and training:
A bachelor's degree is typically required, although some employers prefer or require a master's degree. Common areas of study include operations research, mathematics, statistics, data science, engineering, or another quantitative field.
Key skills and abilities required:
Mathematics
Active listening
Critical thinking
Reading comprehension
Speaking
O*NET also identifies complex problem-solving, judgment and decision-making, systems analysis, systems evaluation, and operations analysis as important transferable skills.
Salary range:
Entry-level: approximately $53,910
Median: $91,290
Experienced: approximately $159,280
The lower and upper figures represent the 10th and 90th percentiles of May 2024 wages.
Job outlook:
Faster than average
Projected growth rate: 21% from 2024–2034.
BLS projects 21% growth and approximately 9,600 openings per year on average.
Industries and employers that hire for this role:
Logistics and supply-chain companies
Manufacturing
Government
Transportation and shipping
Food, agriculture, and retail companies

It may also involve less direct AI development than a data science or software engineering career, so I would need to find opportunities to combine operations research with AI and machine learning.
My T-Shape for this career:
Deep expertise — vertical bar:

Adjacent skills — horizontal bar:

Overall Career Exploration Reflection
After comparing these three careers, I am most interested in the intersection between AI, data, and solving hunger. Software development would give me the strongest ability to build an actual application, while data science would allow me to use AI and data to identify patterns and predict problems. Operations research is especially interesting because it connects technology with logistics, optimization, and resource distribution, which could be directly related to reducing hunger and food waste.
The career that currently stands out most to me is Data Scientist, because it combines AI, technology, creativity, and problem-solving while giving me many ways to work on meaningful problems. However, I also want to continue exploring Operations Research because it could allow me to use AI and analytical tools to improve food distribution and supply chains without requiring me to spend all of my time programming. Software development remains important because understanding how to build technology could help me eventually create my own solution to hunger.
My overall career goal is not simply to get a job working with AI. I want to develop enough knowledge of AI, data, technology, and real-world systems to eventually create or contribute to a solution that helps reduce hunger, food waste, or unequal access to food. These three careers show me that there are multiple paths I could take to work toward that goal.
